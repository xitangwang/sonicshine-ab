Cleaning, rinsing and drying: design the complete process

A useful process definition follows the part beyond the ultrasonic stage. Specify the outgoing condition, stage transfers and the point at which the part is accepted.

Published / updated 2026-11-09Editorial guidance3 min read

Quick answer

Removing contamination in a cleaning bath is one part of a production sequence. The condition delivered to assembly, inspection or packaging depends on what happens afterward as well.

1. Define the condition at handover

Describe what the next operation needs to receive. Is the part transferred immediately to assembly, held for inspection or packed for transport? Explain whether residual moisture, visible marks or handling contamination could interfere with that operation.

This description should be specific enough to guide a discussion without prescribing an untested solution. If a part must reach a defined state before packaging, record that requirement and agree how it will be checked. Avoid using “clean and dry” as a substitute for an acceptance method.

2. Give each stage a job

Elma’s process overview treats ultrasonic cleaning as part of a broader sequence that can include rinsing and drying. That is a useful starting point for thinking about stage functions, rather than choosing a tank count first.

Write a short purpose statement for each proposed stage: what enters it, what it is intended to change and what condition is required at exit. Where rinsing is considered, discuss the liquid supply, carry-over and how its condition will be managed. Where drying is considered, discuss the geometry and the required outgoing state.

3. Treat transfers as part of the process

Map how the basket moves between stages, including pauses and drain positions. A transfer can carry liquid into the next stage or expose a part to handling. A process diagram should therefore show both the treatment stages and the movement between them.

Part geometry matters here. Recesses, nested features and hollow components may need an orientation review to understand drainage. Record the arrangement used in an evaluation so that a later change in loading or transfer method is visible.

4. Build a stage review matrix

Use the matrix to identify open decisions. It helps explain why two machines with a similar number of tanks may represent different process scopes. A tank count is useful only after its intended functions are understood.

Process areaQuestion for the technical review
CleaningWhat residue is being addressed, on which material and geometry?
TransferHow is the basket moved and where does carried liquid drain?
RinsingWhat outgoing condition and liquid-management approach are required?
DryingWhich features retain moisture and how is dryness assessed?
HandoverHow are parts protected until the next operation?

5. Evaluate the whole sequence

Arrange the assessment around the final handover condition. Record the part, load, sequence and checks used. If the process includes rinsing and drying, an inspection taken only at the cleaning-tank exit leaves the later stages unevaluated.

Equipment selection becomes clearer when the discussion follows the part all the way through. Start with the required output, assign a purpose to each stage, and confirm the handling and acceptance method before treating a multi-tank layout as a complete answer.
